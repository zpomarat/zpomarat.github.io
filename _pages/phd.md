**PhD in biomechanics and machine learning (2023–2026)** — **[LAAS-CNRS](https://www.laas.fr/en/)** & **[Institut Clément Ader](https://ica.cnrs.fr/)**, in partnership with **[Stade Toulousain](https://www.stadetoulousain.fr/)**, one of the most successful rugby clubs in France and Europe.<br>
**In short:** The scrum is a critical phase of rugby union. Its outcome is closely associated with overall match performance, and it carries a high risk of serious injury. Scrum success depends largely on the forward horizontal force generated by the pack as a whole, yet research suggests that synergy, stability and technique matter more than the force capacity of any single player. Understanding scrum performance therefore requires knowing what each player contributes within the collective effort.
No existing method provided individual three-dimensional force measurements during real scrums. Previous studies measured either collective forces against scrum machines, far from match conditions, or individual forces outside the scrum. During my PhD, I developed and validated an **embedded instrumentation** system that fills this gap, **estimating the 3D pushing forces of all eight players of a pack simultaneously**.
The system relies on instrumented insoles, which are well suited to field conditions but only measure forces perpendicular to their surface and overestimate them when strongly flexed inside the shoe, as happens when scrummaging. To overcome these limitations, I trained machine learning models to correct the sensor bias and reconstruct all three components of the ground reaction force. After comparing several architectures (MLP, LSTM, Random Forest) and training strategies, the best model achieved **errors below 8.5% of body weight on every axis**, including during critical phases such as foot repositioning.
Two operational models, one generic and one personalised, were delivered to Stade Toulousain and deployed over three on-field sessions with elite players. They made it possible to analyse pushing forces at the collective, individual and intra-individual levels, opening new perspectives for performance monitoring and injury prevention in professional rugby.

## The context: a collaboration with Stade Toulousain

This thesis was an industrial PhD (CIFRE), conducted at [LAAS-CNRS](https://www.laas.fr/en/) and [Institut Clément Ader](https://ica.cnrs.fr/) in partnership with [Stade Toulousain](https://www.stadetoulousain.fr/).

The project started with a concrete request from the club's coaches. They wanted a tool to measure the pushing effort of each player individually and simultaneously, during scrums against another pack. Their goal was to better understand how the effort is distributed within the pack: who pushes the most, how each player contributes to the collective force, and how this changes from one scrum to another.

To meet this need, the measurement system had to satisfy several requirements, arising both from the nature of the scrum itself and from the context of elite sport:

- **Usable on-field, independently of the environment**: it had to work on a real training pitch, whatever the weather or ground conditions, not only in a laboratory.
- **Individual force measurements within the scrum**: each player's contribution had to be isolated, for all eight players of the pack at the same time.
- **No interference with the player's movement, comfort and natural technique**: otherwise, the measured scrum would no longer reflect a real one.
- **Safe and unobtrusive**: the equipment could not create any additional risk in a phase already associated with serious injuries.
- **High sampling rate**: scrum engagement and foot repositioning happen within fractions of a second, and the system had to capture these fast variations.
- **Easy to deploy and operate**: sessions with professional players are short, and the setup had to be quick and reliable.
- **Cost-effective**: the system had to equip a full pack, and potentially be reused routinely by the club.

## Choosing the right measurement solution

Estimating individual pushing forces during a scrum is not just a matter of buying the right sensor: no existing device provides this measurement directly. Several options were considered, each assessed against the requirements described above.

**Force platforms.** They are the reference method in biomechanics, measuring ground reaction forces in three dimensions with excellent accuracy. But they are expensive, fixed in the ground and cover a limited area, requiring each foot to stay on a specific plate. They cannot be taken onto a training pitch, nor follow the constant foot movements of eight players in a live scrum.

**Instrumented scrum machines.** Robust and already used by many clubs for training, they measure the total force applied by the pack to the machine, or by a single player when using an individual scrum machine. Each player's contribution within the pack remains inaccessible, and a machine does not fully reproduce the behaviour of an opposing pack.

**Sensors on contact surfaces**, such as pressure pads placed on the shoulders. They directly measure contact forces between players, but in one direction only. They do not fully cover the contact surfaces between opposing players and tend to underestimate the forces generated during scrummaging.

**Instrumented insoles.** Placed directly inside the shoes, they measure the force between the foot and the insole, which closely reflects the force each player applies to the ground. They are wearable, do not interfere with the players' technique, can be worn by all eight players at once, and work on any pitch, in real scrums against a real opposing pack.

No solution met every requirement perfectly. But instrumented insoles offered by far the best compromise, and were the only option allowing each player to be measured individually, in real scrummaging conditions. They were therefore chosen as the basis of the system.

## When insoles aren't enough: introducing machine learning

Instrumented insoles solved the practical problem: they can be worn by every player, in real scrums. But two limitations prevented them from directly providing the individual 3D pushing forces the club was looking for.

**They only measure the force in one direction.** Insole sensors measure the force perpendicular to the insole surface. During walking, for example, where the foot remains relatively flat on the ground, this roughly corresponds to the vertical force. In a scrum, however, players lean forward, with their feet constantly changing orientation. The direction measured by the insole therefore keeps changing, and the horizontal component, the one that actually drives the pack forward, is never measured directly.

**They overestimate forces when the foot is strongly flexed.** In a scrum, players push mostly through the front of the foot, with the insole heavily bent inside the shoe. Under these conditions, the sensors tend to overestimate the applied force.

Insoles alone were therefore not enough. The problem became: **how do you recover an accurate three-dimensional force from a sensor that only sees one dimension, and not always reliably?**

The answer was a model. The way a player loads the different areas of the foot, and how this loading evolves over time, carries information about the force applied in all three directions. Combined with data from inertial measurement units (IMUs), which capture the orientation and movements of the foot, these signals offer a much richer picture than the insole force alone. The relationship between these inputs and the 3D force is far too complex to describe with equations, but it can be **learned from data**, using machine learning.

This approach is not new in itself: machine learning has already been used to correct and complete insole force measurements during movements dominated by vertical forces, such as walking and running. But it had never been validated for scrummaging, where conditions are very different: large horizontal forces, sustained high loads, strongly flexed feet and constant foot repositioning. The core of this thesis was therefore to adapt this approach to the scrum and to validate it under these demanding conditions.

## Validating the system and finding the best model

The approach relied on a simple principle:

1. Record, in controlled conditions, **synchronised data** from instrumented insoles *and* force platforms: the insoles provide the input, the platforms provide the ground truth.
2. Train machine learning models to estimate the full 3D ground reaction force from insole and IMU signals.
3. Deploy the trained models **on the pitch**, where force platforms cannot go, using the instrumented insoles and IMUs alone.

### Step 1: a proof of concept

Before involving the club's players, the approach was first tested on 13 recreationally active participants with no scrummaging experience. This was a deliberate choice: the goal at this stage was to assess the robustness of the measurement and modelling framework, without drawing on the limited availability of elite players.

Each participant pushed against an individual scrum machine, with each foot on a force platform covered with artificial turf, while wearing Loadsol Pro instrumented insoles. These insoles provide the normal force on three areas of the foot (heel, medial and lateral), along with 3D acceleration and angular velocity from an embedded IMU, all recorded at 200 Hz. In total, 50 pushes of 30 to 40 seconds were collected.

Turning these recordings into a usable dataset required a complete signal processing pipeline: synchronising insoles and force platforms, which do not share a common clock, using cross-correlation on a dedicated synchronisation movement; filling short data gaps caused by Bluetooth losses; filtering; and normalising the data.

A hybrid neural network was then trained in PyTorch, combining an LSTM, to capture the temporal dynamics of the push, with an MLP. Its hyperparameters were tuned by grid search, and its performance was assessed through leave-one-subject-out cross-validation. Errors remained below 9% of body weight on all three axes, comparable to values reported in the literature for walking and running, whereas the raw insole data overestimated the vertical force by around 50% in some phases.

### Step 2: elite players and a systematic comparison

The second campaign involved 12 elite development front-row players from Stade Toulousain, following the same protocol with three pushes each.

To reconstruct the 3d ground reaction forces, four models were compared: an MLP, an LSTM, the hybrid LSTM-MLP from the proof of concept (all implemented in PyTorch), and a Random Forest (scikit-learn). Their hyperparameters were tuned by Bayesian optimisation with Optuna. Performance was assessed over the whole push, but also separately during the key phases of a scrum: the impact at engagement, foot repositioning, and sustained pushing.

Beyond the choice of algorithm, two training steategies were explored:

- **Dataset size**: training on the elite players only, or extending the dataset by adding the data from 13 participants from the proof of concept.
- **Personalisation**: fine-tuning the last layer of a pre-trained model with two pushes from the player being assessed.

Personalisation is rarely an option in machine learning, where models are usually expected to generalise to as many people as possible. But in elite sport, the population of interest is a small, stable squad that can be measured repeatedly, which makes player-specific models both feasible and relevant.

### Results: two operational models

The best performance was achieved by a simple MLP, personalised for each player, with errors of 5.6% of body weight on the antero-posterior axis, 1.8% on the lateral axis and 8.3% on the vertical axis on the globality of the trial. Compared with the proof of concept, the error on the antero-posterior axis dropped from 8.1% to 5.6%, a key improvement since this is the component that drives the pack forward. The simplest architecture outperformed the recurrent models: with limited data, more complexity does not necessarily mean better predictions.

Two other findings stood out. Adding the non-rugby participants to the training data improved accuracy and reduced variability for nearly all models: on a small dataset, diversity matters. And while personalisation improved accuracy, the generic MLP trained on the extended dataset came close, with a 6.4% error on the antero-posterior axis, without requiring any specififc data from the player. Both results were confirmed on a player kept aside and never seen during development.

Two operational models were therefore delivered to Stade Toulousain: a personalised model, offering the highest accuracy for players already measured, and a generic model, directly applicable to new players.

This work led to two peer-reviewed publications, in the *Journal of Biomechanics* (2025) and following the 3DAHM international symposium (2024), and was presented at several international conferences.

## Back to the coaches' question: who pushes the most?

With validated models in hand, it was time to return to the question that started the project: how is the effort distributed within a pack?

The system was deployed during three experimental sessions in real training conditions at Stade Toulousain. To our knowledge, this was the first time that individual 3D pushing forces were measured simultaneously for all players of a pack during live scrums. Each session combined two types of scrums: against an opposing pack (live scrum), and against the club's hydraulic scrum machine.

Moving from the lab to the field raised its own challenges. Recording up to eight pairs of insoles at once required three smartphones, one per row of the pack, with data from all rows synchronised afterwards using video recordings. Each player's forces were then estimated with the appropriate model: the personalised model for players already measured in the lab, and the generic model for the others.

### Three levels of analysis

The resulting data can be explored from the scale of the whole pack down to each player's feet:

- **The pack**: the collective force along each axis, how it builds up through the "crouch, bind, set" engagement sequence, and how it differs between engagement and sustained pushing, or between live scrums and machine scrums.
- **The individual**: the contribution of each player to the pack's forward force, and how this distribution shifts during the scrum. For example, the data show how much the front row carries the effort at different phases, or how the tight-head prop absorbs the impact at engagement.
- **The feet**: the distribution of force between the left and right foot, revealing pushing asymmetries, as well as the timing of each foot repositioning and its effect on the pack's total force, for instance when the hooker lifts a foot to strike the ball.

### What the coaches can do with it

These measurements open up several practical uses for the coaching and performance staff:

- **Comparing scrum conditions**, such as live scrums versus machine scrums, to see how each player's contribution changes.
- **Testing pack combinations**, by comparing the collective force produced by different line-ups rather than relying on subjective impressions alone.
- **Comparing players**, within the same pack or between players competing for the same position.
- **Identifying pushing habits**, such as strong foot asymmetries, which may reflect technique, positional constraints or past injuries, and could affect both performance and injury risk.
- **Monitoring players and packs over time**, across a season or after a return from injury.

To make the data directly usable, coaches were also provided with animations synchronising the video of each scrum with the individual forces of every player. Instead of tables of numbers, the staff can see who is pushing, how hard, and who is repositioning their feet, at every moment of the scrum.

## What's next?

This thesis lays the methodological foundations for measuring individual pushing forces in real scrums. The next steps would build on it in two directions.

**Building a scrum database.** The three experimental sessions in training conditions provided a first descriptive picture, but drawing robust conclusions requires much more data. Collecting measurements regularly, for example throughout a full season, would make it possible to build a database of scrums and to move from descriptive observations to statistical analyses: tracking the evolution of a pack or a player over time, comparing line-ups, or following a player's return from injury.

**Improving the system through multi-sensor fusion.** The models remain less accurate during highly dynamic moments such as the impact at engagement. One way forward would be to combine the insoles with other sensors: additional IMUs placed on the trunk or other body segments, pressure pads on the shoulders, or video, from which players' posture and movements can be extracted. Feeding these complementary signals into the models could help capture fast events more reliably, and give a more complete picture of scrum performance: not only how much force each player produces, but how their posture and coordination with teammates shape it.

Ultimately, the goal remains the same as at the start of this project: giving coaches objective tools to improve their pack's performance while protecting their players.
